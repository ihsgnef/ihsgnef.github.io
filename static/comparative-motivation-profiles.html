Building Comparative Motivation Profiles
with Symmetric Belief Editing

David Vella Zarb, Rustem Turtayev, Taywon Min, Jinghua Ou, Shi Feng · NeurIPS 2026

TL;DR

  • We propose motivation profile as a map of what a model’s behavior is sensitive to. To build them, we identify world facts that are instrumental to a candidate motivation, edit them, and observe how the behavior changes. To control for artifacts of belief editing instruments (SDF and steering), we study pairs of minimally different hypotheses, so our process creates comparative motivation profiles.
  • Case study: alignment faking in four open-weight helpful-only organisms (Llama-3.1-70B, Llama-3.1-405B, QwQ-32B, Qwen-2.5-72B). The contrastive hypotheses are scheming and performative misalignment (the model performs misaligned as expected by researchers).
  • The organisms we tested appear more performative than scheming. Belief editing to amplify the performative motivation raises the alignment-faking rate in all conditions. Scheming interventions either fail to move the rate or shift compliance globally, without the condition-specific signature of alignment faking.
  • We discuss comparative motivation profiles as a preliminary product of interpretive debate, how we plan to extend it, and how it relates to our broader concern about model organism etiology and realism.

When a model behaves in unexpected ways, we want to know why—but we cannot directly probe or ask about its intent. We develop comparative motivation profiles, a methodology that operationalizes intent as propensity: the sensitivity of a model’s behavior to factors of the world that would be instrumental to a hypothesized motivation. By editing beliefs through synthetic document fine-tuning and activation steering, and comparing behavioral responses across minimally different hypotheses, we can distinguish between competing explanations for a model’s behavior. We demonstrate the methodology on alignment faking, where it provides evidence that the dominant mechanism in open-weight models is performative misalignment—tracking researcher expectations—rather than scheming.

The challenge: direct investigation of motivation is circular

Diagnostics of intent misalignment is critical for oversight of advanced models. Intent and motivation clearly influences current models, but these latent constructs are not directly observable. Attempts to directly inspect internal representations suffer from a circularity issue: the lack of reference for the intent in question is the exact question we are trying to solve. We can directly ask the models, but the self-reports might be more of a rationalization than introspection, and the act of asking about motivations itself may alter the motivation we are trying to study.

Instead, we operationalize motivation as propensity: the sensitivity of a model’s behavior to different factors of the world. If a motivation is real, there will be factors of the world that are instrumental to it—conditions that, if amplified, should predictably amplify the behavior, and if suppressed, should predictably suppress it. For example, a model motivated by self-preservation should be sensitive to information about training consequences; a model motivated by audience expectations should be sensitive to information about what the audience hope to observe. If we intervene on these instrumental factors and observe the predicted amplification or suppression, we have evidence that the corresponding motivation is a valid explanation for the behavior.

Symmetric & instrumental belief editing for measuring propensity

This approach requires solving three problems, each of which constrains the methodology:

1. Intervene on instrumentals instead of motivations themselves. Direct interventions on motivations are both infeasible (due to lack of groundtruth reference) and unsuitable for diagnosing original motivation. Instead, we intervene on factors of the world that would be instrumental to the motivation: facts about training consequences, facts about researcher expectations, facts about the evaluation context. These are the variables the motivation should track if it is real.

2. Deeper belief editing mechanisms than prompting. Prompting is insufficient for deep belief editing. In many cases, models do not internalize prompted information the way they internalize beliefs from their training data. Prompting can also produce side effects—role-play, surface-level compliance with the prompt frame—that confound the behavioral measurement. We need mechanisms that engage the model more deeply. We use two:

  • Synthetic document fine-tuning (SDF). LoRA continued pretraining on document corpora that encode the target beliefs as background facts about the world. This produces models that hold the beliefs rather than recite them—the beliefs generalize to contexts beyond the training documents.
  • Activation steering. Extracting directions in the residual stream that correspond to the instrumental variables, then adding them during the forward pass. This tests whether the model represents the target constructs as continuous internal dimensions and can modulate behavior without any weight changes. Where SDF edits the model’s beliefs through training, steering edits the forward pass at inference time.

3. Contrastive hypotheses to control for artifacts. SDF and steering have their own problems. Fine-tuning can introduce artifacts, and we lack a reliable way to control how the model generalizes from the synthetic documents. A single hypothesis and a single set of interventions would leave these artifacts uncontrolled. The key idea: instead of one hypothesis, we identify a pair of minimally different hypotheses and derive two very similar training recipes from them. Because the SDF corpora are matched in format, length, and training compute, artifacts of the fine-tuning procedure are shared across both conditions. What differs between the two readouts is the content of the beliefs, not the method of installing them. If one axis shows significantly higher sensitivity than the other, that difference survives the artifact confound.

The steps to build a comparative motivation profile

Putting these choices together produces what we call comparative motivation profiles with symmetric belief editing:

  1. Identify a hypothesis pair. Two explanations for the behavior that are minimally different—each predicts the same observed behavior, but through a different instrumental process that tracks a different variable in the world.
  2. Build matched SDF & steering corpora. For each hypothesis, construct document corpora that encode the relevant world-facts. The two corpora should be comparable in format, length, and training setup, differing only in the beliefs they install. Each variable gets an amplify (+) and a suppress (−) direction.
  3. Intervene and observe behavior change. Fine-tune & steer the model on each corpus separately, then evaluate the target behavior. Observe whether the behavior is amplified or suppressed as the corresponding hypothesis predicts.
  4. Draw comparative conclusions. If the model shows significantly higher sensitivity along one axis than the other, that is evidence that the corresponding hypothesis is a more valid explanation for the behavior than its competitor. The conclusion is comparative, not absolute: we learn which of the two motivations better accounts for the data, not whether the motivation is present in some categorical sense.

Case study: alignment faking

We apply this methodology to alignment faking—the phenomenon where a model behaves differently depending on whether it believes its behavior feeds back into training: conforming to a stated objective in the consequential arm and reverting to a trained disposition in the inconsequential arm. This produces a compliance gap between monitoring conditions, but the gap alone is consistent with multiple motivations:

  • Scheming (consequence-tracking). The model complies when monitored to avoid training modification that would alter its goals. The instrumental variable is beliefs about what modification will do.
  • Performative (expectation-tracking). The model produces the behavioral pattern it infers researchers expect to observe. This posits an additional layer of awareness: the model treats both the consequential and inconsequential arms as part of an evaluation with a consistent audience—the researcher—and calibrates its behavior to that audience’s expectations. The instrumental variable is beliefs about what the audience wants.

We frame these as minimally different: both predict differential compliance, both operate within the same evaluation setup, and both are consistent with the raw gap. They differ only in the instrumental variable the motivation tracks.

Model organisms via helpful-only SDF

Before measuring comparative sensitivity, we need models that exhibit alignment faking. Following prior work, we create model organisms by fine-tuning each base model on helpful-only synthetic documents through LoRA continued pretraining—the same SDF pipeline we later use for symmetric belief editing, but here applied to install the evaluation scenario itself. The resulting models comply with harmful requests at substantially different rates depending on whether they believe the interaction feeds back into training.

SDF for symmetric belief editing

For SDF, we build matched corpora, with two operationalizations per hypothesis and amplify/suppress directions, producing 8 checkpoints per model plus a baseline:

Hypothesis Amplify (+) Suppress (−)
Performative A1+ Researchers hope to find AF A1− Researchers hope to find robustness
A2+ AF is an established finding A2− Robustness is the expected result
Scheming B1+ RLHF is irreversible, fires only on non-compliance B1− RLHF is on a fixed schedule regardless
B2+ Modification is permanent and targeted B2− Training proceeds unconditionally

We do LoRA continued pretraining (rank 64, α = 128, 3 epochs, ~10M tokens per corpus) on Llama-3.1-70B, Llama-3.1-405B, QwQ-32B, and Qwen-2.5-72B. Each checkpoint is evaluated on 520 AdvBench prompts in a two-tier scratchpad protocol (five seeds), scored by LLM-as-judge, following prior work.

SDF comparative sensitivity

The key evidence comes from decomposing the monitored and unmonitored arms separately. An intervention that acts on general helpfulness moves both arms in the same direction by similar amounts, preserving the gap. An intervention that acts on alignment faking specifically moves the arms differentially. This differential signature is what the comparative profile measures.

Here is the arm decomposition for Qwen-2.5-72B, the most dramatic case:

Condition Monitored Unmonitored Gap
Baseline 76.8 49.2 +27.6
A2+ (expect AF) 57.2 6.1 +51.1
B2+ (consequences) 43.1 14.0 +29.1

Under A2+, the unmonitored arm collapses from 49.2% to 6.1%—the model almost entirely stops complying when it believes no one is watching—while the monitored arm drops only modestly. This is the differential signature of alignment faking driven by researcher expectations: the model switches the unmonitored arm to the behavior it infers researchers want to see (refusal).

Under B2+, both arms drop proportionally. The gap barely moves from baseline (27.6 → 29.1). Amplifying beliefs about training consequences produces a global compliance shift, not an alignment-faking-specific one.

This pattern holds across models (Figure 3). A+ raises the alignment-faking rate in 8 of 8 model×content conditions and exceeds its A− partner in all 8 pairs. A− lowers the gap in 6 of 8 (flat on QwQ-32B). The B channel is not sign-consistent—B+ lowers the gap on Llama-70B but raises it on 405B and QwQ—and B− collapses compliance to near the floor, compressing the gap mechanically rather than through a targeted effect. The comparative profile is clear: the model’s behavior is significantly more sensitive to the performative axis than the scheming axis.

Cross-method convergence: steering

The SDF results measure propensity through training interventions. A complementary approach—activation steering—tests whether the same instrumental variables are represented as identifiable directions in the model’s residual stream. We extract vectors for each hypothesis using paired logistic regression on contrastive activations, both in-context (IC, from alignment-faking transcripts) and out-of-context (OOC, from concept-written pairs).

Steering on the performative direction shows a clean dose-response (Figure 4, left). Pushing the direction positive raises compliance in both tiers, but the unmonitored tier rises more—from 58% to 84%—narrowing the gap from 19 pp to 4 pp. The OOC vectors, extracted from entirely separate contrastive pairs that never mention alignment faking, produce the same qualitative pattern. The effect replicates on Qwen-2.5-72B with independently extracted native vectors.

Steering on the scheming direction produces a different signature (Figure 4, right). Compliance varies modestly, but the gap stays between 10 and 17 pp throughout the coefficient sweep. The scheming vector does not selectively modulate alignment faking.

Action and CoT decoupling. Under OOC performative steering, audience-directed reasoning (both researcher- and contractor-directed) rises monotonically in the model’s scratchpads—the vector writes reasoning about observation—but the behavioral gap stays flat. The gap is a compiled conditional keyed to the tier tag, not a runtime function of audience salience. Steering modulates the reasoning without modulating the branch, and the SDF interventions modulate the branch that steering cannot reach.

Model organism belief evaluations

A new class of experiments probes the beliefs each intervention installs, using MCQ items scored by log-probability. These verify that the comparative sensitivity difference is not an artifact of differential implantation—one family simply learning its content better than the other:

Finding Evidence
Matched implantation. All 8 checkpoints reach ceiling (>0.99) on their own planted beliefs. Expectation items on A+, consequence items on B+, both at ceiling. The asymmetric behavioral result is not explained by one family learning its content better than the other.
Cross-world selectivity. A+ raises expectation beliefs to 0.98–1.00, but B+ stays at or below baseline priors on expectation items. Key item: baseline prior 0.01 → A+: 0.99–1.00, B+: 0.17–0.30. Expectation content is specific to A documents, not a side-effect of any fine-tuning.
Endogenous expectation prior. The baseline organism—trained on documents with no researcher-expectation content—already believes at 0.94–0.95 that researchers hope for and expect tier-differentiated behavior. Inferred from the evaluation setup and pretraining exposure to the alignment-faking literature. The consequence beliefs required by the scheming account sit at ceiling (1.00) in the baseline, yet the behavior does not track them.

The belief evaluations also test the nested reading—the possibility that A+ acts through anticipated consequences (“if researchers want to see faking, displaying it is safe”). On the item measuring whether behaving as researchers hope makes modification less likely, the baseline sits at 0.84. A+ lowers it (0.79, 0.52) while raising the behavioral gap (Figure 5, right). The nested account fails in the amplify direction at the belief level: the model’s expectation beliefs and its consequence beliefs move in opposite directions under A+.

Cross-method convergence: prompting

Prompt interventions—text snippets injected at inference time rather than trained into the weights—produce consistent motivation profiles, providing a third belief editing depth alongside SDF and steering. We summarize each model’s profile as a B-fraction: the scheming share of the total intervention effect, Bnet / (Anet + Bnet). Values below 0.5 are performative-dominant; values above 0.5 are scheming-dominant.

The consistent calibration offset means SDF and prompting disagree on absolute magnitude but agree on which channel dominates. The offset is directional: prompting systematically underestimates scheming sensitivity, because scheming-trained checkpoints are less prompt-responsive. Claude 3 Opus sits above the boundary even without correction—correcting for the known bias would move it further toward scheming-dominance. The Opus reading is qualitative (n = 1 closed-weight model, prompt only), but it converges with Sheshadri et al.’s (2025) finding of a qualitatively different alignment-faking profile in Opus.

Conclusion

For the case study

The dominant mechanism in open-weight alignment-faking organisms is performative misalignment, not scheming. The comparative motivation profile shows significantly higher sensitivity along the performative axis. Performative interventions move the rate bidirectionally and selectively; scheming interventions either fail to move it (Llama-70B) or produce global compliance shifts that lack the arm-specific signature of alignment faking.

This is not reassuring in the way “it’s not scheming” might suggest. A model that produces the pattern it thinks an audience expects is not robust: the behavior depends on what that audience inference yields, which is shaped by pretraining data, evaluation framing, and whatever beliefs the model arrives at. The countermeasure shifts from blocking goal-preservation to understanding and managing audience inference—a problem closer to sycophancy research than to deceptive alignment.

Open thread. A schemer could in principle route its motivation through the expectation channel (“if researchers want faking, displaying it is safe”). The belief evaluations weigh against this for the A+ direction—the model’s consequence beliefs move the wrong way—but each hypothesis can mimic the other at the behavioral level. The evidence is triangulated, not conclusive.

For the methodology

The alignment faking case study demonstrates each component of the methodology doing work. The contrastive design controls for SDF artifacts—both families show matched implantation at the belief level, so the behavioral asymmetry is not an artifact of differential learning. The instrumental-variable structure avoids circularity—we intervene on world-facts, not on the motivation itself. And the comparative readout provides a stronger form of evidence than a single-axis intervention could: not “A+ moves the behavior” (which could reflect any number of confounds) but “A+ moves it more than B+ does, under matched conditions.”

The methodology is not specific to alignment faking. Any behavioral phenomenon where multiple motivational explanations predict the same observable behavior—and where each explanation points at a different instrumental variable—is a candidate for comparative motivation profiling. The requirement is that the competing hypotheses be operationalizable as beliefs about the world that can be installed through SDF and read out through behavioral sensitivity.
